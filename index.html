1 · Conditional system: read the reality first

Zero · First · Second · Third

Conditionals are not just verb formulas—they express different levels of reality.

TypeMain meaningCore patternExample
ZeroGeneral truth / repeated resultif + Present, PresentIf you heat ice, it melts.
FirstReal / possible futureif + Present, will + base verbIf I finish early, I'll call you.
SecondHypothetical / unlikely present-futureif + Past, would + base verbIf I had more time, I would study more.
ThirdImpossible past / regretif + Past Perfect, would have + ppIf I had studied, I would have passed.
FactZero
Possible futureFirst
Unreal nowSecond
Unreal pastThird
Common trap: Do not choose the conditional from the verb form alone. First decide whether the situation is general, possible, unreal now, or impossible because the past is finished.
Exam strategy: Ask: Is this always true? Is the future still possible? Is it hypothetical now? Is the speaker imagining a different past?

Quick Check · 3 items

1. If water ___ 100°C, it boils.
2. If I finish early, I ___ you.
3. If I had more free time, I ___ another language.

2 · Zero Conditional

Facts, rules and predictable results

Use Zero Conditional when the result is generally true whenever the condition happens.

If + Present Simple, Present Simple
If you don't water plants, they die.
If people exercise, they often feel better.
If I drink coffee late, I sleep badly.
Ice melts if you heat it.

Word order can change.

The if-clause can come first or second. When it comes first, use a comma.

If you press this button, the machine stops.
The machine stops if you press this button.
Common mistakes: using will for a general truth, or using the Zero Conditional for a one-time future event.
Exam strategy: Look for habits, scientific facts, instructions, routines, or results that happen repeatedly.

Quick Check · 3 items

1. If you ___ metal, it expands.
2. Plants die if they ___ enough water.
3. Which sentence is Zero Conditional?

3 · First Conditional

Real and possible future situations

Use First Conditional when the condition is realistic and the future result is still possible.

If + Present Simple, will + base verb
If I have time, I will help you.
If it rains, we will stay home.
She will pass if she studies.
If they don't leave now, they will miss the bus.

The result clause does not have to use only will.

Depending on meaning, the result can contain a modal or imperative.

If you're tired, you should rest.
If you see Carlos, tell him to call me.
Common mistake: If it will rain, we'll stay home. ✗ → If it rains, we'll stay home.
Exam strategy: In the if-clause, future meaning normally uses Present Simple, not will.

Quick Check · 3 items

1. If she ___ tonight, she'll finish on time.
2. If we leave now, we ___ the train.
3. If you see Marco, ___ him this message.

4 · Second Conditional

Hypothetical or unlikely present/future situations

Use Second Conditional when the situation is unreal, imaginary, or unlikely now or in the future.

If + Simple Past, would + base verb
If I had more money, I would travel more.
If she lived closer, we would meet more often.
What would you do if you lost your phone?
If I were you, I would wait.

Were is especially important in hypothetical statements.

In formal and exam English, If I were you is the preferred pattern for advice.

Common mistakes: If I would have more money... ✗ · If I will have more money... ✗
Exam strategy: If the speaker is imagining a different present or unlikely future, look for Past Simple + would.

Quick Check · 3 items

1. If I ___ you, I'd accept the offer.
2. If he had more experience, he ___ for the position.
3. What would you do if you ___ a year off?

5 · Third Conditional

Imagining a different past

Use Third Conditional for past situations that did not happen and their imagined consequences.

If + Past Perfect, would have + past participle
If I had left earlier, I would have arrived on time.
If she had studied, she would have passed.
They wouldn't have missed the flight if they had checked the time.
What would you have done if you had known?

Past Perfect marks the unreal past condition.

The result uses would have + past participle. Both parts refer to a past that cannot now be changed.

Common mistakes: If I would have known... ✗ · If I had knew... ✗ · Correct: If I had known...
Exam strategy: Look for a finished past event and a consequence that is also impossible to change now.

Quick Check · 3 items

1. If they had left earlier, they ___ the traffic.
2. If I ___ about the meeting, I would have attended.
3. Which sentence is correct?

6 · Unless & conditional connectors

unless · if not · as long as · provided that

Unless means if not.

You won't improve unless you practice.
= You won't improve if you don't practice.
We can go as long as we finish first.
You may borrow it provided that you return it tomorrow.
Common trap: unless you don't study often reverses the intended meaning. Unless already contains the negative idea.
Exam strategy: Translate the connector into logic: unless = if not; as long as / provided that = only if this condition is satisfied.

Quick Check · 3 items

1. You won't pass ___ you study.
2. We can leave early ___ we finish everything first.
3. Which is equivalent to 'If you don't hurry, you'll miss the bus'?

7 · I wish: present situations

Wish + Simple Past

Use I wish + Simple Past when you want the present situation to be different.

wish + Simple Past
I wish I had more time.
She wishes she lived closer.
I wish it weren't so cold.
We wish we could stay longer.

Past form, present meaning

The past form shows distance from reality. The sentence is about now, not the past.

Common trap: I wish I have more time is not the normal structure for an unreal present wish.
Exam strategy: If the regret/desire is about now, expect a past form after wish.

Quick Check · 3 items

1. I wish I ___ more free time.
2. She wishes she ___ speak French.
3. I wish it ___ raining.

8 · I wish / If only: past regret

Wish + Past Perfect

Use wish / if only + Past Perfect for regret about a finished past situation.

wish / if only + had + past participle
I wish I had studied more.
She wishes she hadn't said that.
If only we had left earlier.
I wish they had told me.

If only is often more emphatic.

The grammar is the same basic regret system, but if only usually sounds stronger or more emotional.

Common mistake: I wish I studied more yesterday. ✗ when expressing regret about a finished past action → I wish I had studied more.
Exam strategy: If the speaker regrets something that is already finished, look for had + past participle.

Quick Check · 3 items

1. I wish I ___ the instructions before starting.
2. If only we ___ earlier!
3. She wishes she ___ that message.

9 · should have + past participle

Past advice, criticism and regret

Should have + past participle means the better action did not happen.

should have + past participle
You should have checked the answer.
I shouldn't have stayed up so late.
They should have called us.
She shouldn't have ignored the warning.

Compare with Third Conditional.

Should have evaluates a past action. Third Conditional imagines a different past condition and consequence.

You should have left earlier. → criticism/advice
If you had left earlier, you would have arrived on time. → hypothetical consequence
Common mistakes: should have went ✗ · Correct: should have gone.
Exam strategy: If the sentence means “that was the better action, but it didn't happen,” choose should have + pp.

Quick Check · 3 items

1. You ___ the email before sending it.
2. I ___ so much coffee last night.
3. Which sentence expresses past criticism?

10 · Regret & hypothetical consequences

Wish · should have · Third Conditional

These structures all talk about situations we would prefer to be different—but they do different jobs.

StructureMain useExample
I wish + PastUnreal presentI wish I had more time.
I wish + Past PerfectPast regretI wish I had studied more.
should have + ppPast advice / criticismYou should have called.
Third ConditionalAlternative past cause + resultIf I had called, she would have known.

Meaning first, structure second.

Exam distractors often use forms that are grammatically familiar but express the wrong relationship in time.

Common trap: Do not confuse I wish I had... (present wish) with I wish I had done... (past regret). The extra past participle changes the time reference.
Exam strategy: Identify two things: time (now or past?) and purpose (wish, criticism, or hypothetical consequence?).

Quick Check · 3 items

1. I wish I ___ more confident when speaking English.
2. I wish I ___ more carefully before the test.
3. If she had asked for help, she ___ the problem sooner.
Week 7 exam habit: Identify the time and reality first. Then choose the structure: fact, real future, unreal present/future, unreal past, present wish, past regret, or past criticism.
